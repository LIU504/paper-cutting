From Paper to Screen: The Digital Preservation of Chinese Paper cutting

A comprehensive digital repository dedicated to documenting the artisanal heritage and cultural significance of jianzhi art during the lunar new year.

About this Website



Why fu and zodiac paper-cuts
In 2009 UNESCO inscribed "Chinese paper-cut" on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. Its entry says paper-cuts are used to decorate windows, beds and ceilings and to mark weddings, birthdays and other celebrations (UNESCO, 2009). Pasting window flowers at Spring Festival is one of these uses; one craftsperson calls it "not just a form, but a culture" (China.org.cn, 2018). The fu character and the zodiac animals are both common Spring Festival subjects, their patterns are easy to recognise, and a lot of online material exists on them. That is why we chose them.

Scope of the background sources
UNESCO's entry covers Chinese paper-cutting as a whole, not just Spring Festival paper-cuts, so we use it only for background. Our analysis of Spring Festival paper-cuts rests on the fu and zodiac works and the cases we collected.

How we selected sources
We preferred sources with a named institution or author, works whose origin can be traced, and works whose source explains their use or meaning. Every claim about meaning is attributed to a source. Our own observations are kept separate.

Why we include works, making materials and cross-regional cases
A collection of finished works does not show how they are made, and a collection of making processes does not show how the paper-cuts are presented to different readers. We need all three to answer what is preserved and what changes when it is shared.

How a digital archive helps preserve culture
UNESCO notes that paper-cutting is mostly passed on within families, often from mother to daughter, starting in childhood (UNESCO, 2009), and one craftsperson says "many people no longer cut" (China.org.cn, 2018). Much of this knowledge lives in people's hands, and an archive cannot replace learning to cut by hand. It can do three smaller things. These are our own judgements, not conclusions from the sources.
- It keeps a work, its occasion of use, its meaning and its source in one record. Window flowers are festival decorations, so the pieces themselves may not last, but a record can last and can be checked.
- It shows what existing sources record and what they leave out. Both fu reports we collected describe the occasion and the meaning, and neither explains the pattern or the cutting method.
- It puts material from different regions and media into one format, which makes comparison easier.

What value the website may offer
Once built, the website may be useful to three groups. These are possible uses only. We have not tested the site with real users.
- Students and teachers learning about Spring Festival or paper-cutting can find sourced works and see what each source actually says.
- Curious general readers, including those unfamiliar with Chinese customs, can see fu and zodiac paper-cuts and how different regions describe them in one place.
- People building similar archives or doing similar research can reuse or compare our selection method, our archive card format and our source list.

Limitations
Our sample is small and does not represent all paper-cuts or all regions.

Making and Digital Documentation

Digital sources record different aspects of paper-cutting. This section compares a photograph of Li Shoubai’s rabbit paper-cut, Scarlett PANG’s illustrated rat tutorial, and Pika Shuwu’s snake video. Together, they show how a finished image, step-by-step illustrations, and moving footage document appearance and technique—and where further explanation is needed (Pika Shuwu, 2020; Scarlett PANG, 2026; Sina, 2023).

What the comparison shows

The photograph allows viewers to examine the finished rabbit design, but it cannot show the order in which the paper was cut. The illustrated tutorial makes the rat paper-cut easier to follow by showing patterns and successive steps, although movement between those steps remains invisible. In the snake video, viewers can observe the maker’s hands while the pattern is drawn and cut. Its webpage description, however, gives no cultural explanation of the snake motif. These observations concern these three sources; they do not mean that photographs or videos can never include such explanations. A caption, interview, or narration could add them. By linking different forms of documentation, this archive gives visitors a fuller view of appearance and technique. Additional information from artists or reliable cultural sources is still needed to explain why the motifs matter.

Cross Cultural Communication



Why compare these cases

A paper-cut can travel, but its meaning may need translation. We compare three presentations from China, the United Kingdom and the United States to see how publishers explain Chinese paper cutting to different audiences. The Chinese and British examples both discuss fish and abundance; the American example adds a comparison of teaching methods through window flowers and the character for Spring. These are related traditions, not identical objects. Our question is how language, background information and opportunities to participate change across these settings.
